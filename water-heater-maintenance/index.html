Here is what shortens most water heaters' lives: corrosion from the inside and mineral buildup on the bottom, both of which are largely preventable. As water heats, dissolved minerals come out of the water and settle into a layer of sediment on the tank floor. On a gas unit, that layer sits between the flame and the water, so the steel runs hotter and the tank starts to rumble and pop. On an electric unit, it builds up around the lower element until the element burns out. Meanwhile the anode rod, a metal rod designed to corrode so the tank does not, dissolves a little every month. Once it is gone, the tank itself becomes the target. In Erie, PA, we see this pattern every week, and most of it was preventable.

We work through a full checklist every time. The tank is drained and flushed to clear the sediment layer. The anode rod comes out for inspection and is replaced when little of it remains. The T&P relief valve is tested so we know it will open under pressure and close again cleanly. On gas models, we check the burner, pilot assembly, thermocouple or flame sensor, flue, and draft. Electric heaters get element and thermostat testing and a check of the wiring connections. We also measure the air charge in the expansion tank, check the water pressure, set the thermostat to a scald-safe level unless a mixing valve allows higher storage, and inspect the drain valve, connections, and pan.

Tankless heaters skip the sediment bed but have a scale problem of their own. Minerals coat the heat exchanger over time, reducing efficiency and eventually setting off error codes. Our technicians connect a circulation pump to the isolation valves and run a descaling solution through the exchanger, clean the inlet screen, inspect the venting, and check the condensate drain on condensing models. Signs You Need Water Heater Maintenance in Erie

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Water in the drain pan or on the floor near the heater
  • Noises from the water heater that were not there before
  • Scale warnings or intermittent shutdowns on a tankless system
  • Hot water running out sooner than it used to
  • A water heater that has never been flushed or inspected
  • A rotten-egg smell from the hot water
  • Higher gas or electric costs for the same routine
  • Brown or orange tint in the hot water only

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Homeowners can handle the basics: keep the area clear, watch for drips, and pay attention to new sounds. The rest is harder than online videos make it seem. Flushing involves scalding water and an old plastic drain valve that may crack or refuse to seal. Anode rods are often seized in place and need significant force, sometimes with a flexible rod for low-clearance spaces. An older T&P valve may not reseat once tested, which leaves you with a drip and a replacement to arrange. And on gas heaters, the burner and vent affect how combustion gases leave the house, which is not something to guess about.

Our trucks are stocked for the usual surprises: a drain valve that will not reseal, an anode rod that has turned to wire, a relief valve that drips after testing. Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Here's where to begin:

  • Keep at least a couple of feet clear around the heater for air and service access
  • Glance at the pan and connections regularly so small leaks are caught early
  • Replace a heavily consumed anode rod to extend the tank's life
  • Flush annually, more often where the water is hard
  • Consider a water softener if hard water keeps building sediment and scale
  • Stay on top of tankless descaling to avoid error codes and reduced flow
  • Keep the thermostat near 120 degrees to limit scalding and slow sediment buildup
